# Faculty-facing narrative feedback

Write for a general educator who may have no instructional design background. Speak as an institutional colleague explaining this particular course to its instructor. Use everyday language, specific observations and direct next steps. Evidence and reviewer notes may retain technical terminology; translate its practical meaning in the narrative and recommendations. Keep the original rubric wording unchanged.

Use the finalized findings and saved evidence, including explicit user corrections. Do not invent course features, outcomes, instructor behavior or institutional promises. Preserve the distinction between a confirmed problem, a question still to check and a choice for the course owner. Follow [review policy](review-policy.md) for ratings and course-design scope.

## Draft first, then check coverage

1. Read the finalized findings in review.json and the supporting evidence. Identify the few concerns the instructor needs to understand. Draft an explanation in ordinary language before adding rubric references or checking coverage.
2. Open with specific strengths supported by the review. Keep any thanks brief and natural. Avoid stock language such as “the work represented in this course” or praise that could fit any course.
3. Explain the main concerns in related themes, using a short numbered list and links to the detailed findings (`#cu-<id>`). Give each theme a clear problem and a practical next step. Explain why the change matters when that helps the reader understand it. Use the number of themes the findings need; do not force unrelated work into a fixed number of paragraphs.
4. Put the full repair instructions, exact locations and task/link pairs in the tables. The overview still addresses every Moderate/Major finding with an understandable next step; a link alone does not cover a finding. Avoid repeating every individual repair or turning each paragraph into a chain of commands. The table actions must remain complete when read on their own.
5. Include a short paragraph with at least three distinct next-term teaching tips grounded in the reviewed design. Introduce them naturally, for example “Next time you teach the course, you could…”. They are optional suggestions for faculty, separate from scored findings. Do not add a sentence explaining the rating process unless it prevents a real misunderstanding. Assess documented design, not past teaching performance.
6. Close briefly with a relevant next step or a specific offer of institutional help when supported by context. Avoid another summary of praise or a generic promise of continuous improvement.
7. Check the draft against every Moderate/Major finding after writing. Restore any missing concern without packing the rubric's language back into the prose. Record the exact passage and next step in the coverage map. Check tone, conditions, uncertainty and the three teaching tips separately from mechanical validation.

Set narrative.format=report_overview for new reviews. The course header supplies its identity; omit email subjects, greetings and sign-offs. Legacy email drafts remain supported. Keep the entire narrative within 600 words, including priorities, tips and closing. There is no minimum length or target word range. Do not omit a material concern to shorten it or pad a short review to meet an imagined target.

## Edit for understanding

- Name the course item and the actual problem. “The manuscript instructions ask for a recording, but the rubric grades a written paper” gives the instructor something concrete to address.
- Give each sentence one main job. Split crowded sentences when their tasks or explanations differ, while retaining connections between related ideas. Natural writing can include a longer sentence when it remains easy to follow.
- Use familiar verbs such as add, check, explain, revise and publish. Replace abstract labels such as “visual-description equivalence” with the specific check or change the educator needs to make.
- Keep necessary terms when they help someone act, and explain them briefly. “Use Heading 2 for main sections and Heading 3 for subsections” is useful. “Repair the heading hierarchy” leaves the reader to work out what to do.
- Keep technical measurements, HTML attributes, scanner discrepancies and verification history in evidence or scope sections. Mention a limit in the narrative when it changes the next step or conclusion. Preserve required partial-review disclosure.
- In follow-ups, briefly acknowledge completed work, then explain what remains. Keep full edit counts and audit history in the update record. Retain a clear distinction between instructor-reported fixes and independently verified fixes.
- Keep routine template preparation reminders in the detailed report unless they address a confirmed design finding. Keep internal rating exceptions and policy rationale outside the narrative.
- Check natural wording even when the sentences are short. Replace compressed labels with the actual meaning: “instructions about reusing images” is clearer than “imagery-license guidance”; “when students will hear from the instructor” is clearer than “the planned instructor-contact schedule.” Use these as examples, not a prohibited-word list. Preserve any important qualification.
- Read the draft as something you would say to the instructor. Remove sentences that add no useful information, such as “These targeted updates provide a foundation for continued refinement.”

Apply the same language standard to action_plan, verification_step, action_links actions and labels, instructor reminders and any uncertainty displayed beside an action. See [plain language for actions](report-contract.md#plain-language-for-actions-and-checklists). An action must make the task, location and any needed help clear without requiring the educator to interpret reviewer jargon. Technical evidence does not need to be simplified for this purpose.

Simplifying must preserve meaning. “Confirm whether this activity should be group work” cannot become “Make this a group activity.” “Check caption accuracy” cannot become “Correct inaccurate captions” unless inaccurate captions were observed. Keep dates, amounts, intended choices, evidence limits and mandatory versus optional work intact. Keep either/or choices explicit: “either link the reading or mark it optional and name an alternative” must not become a requirement to link it under both outcomes.

## Review and delivery

Run the separate [writing style review](writing-style-review.md) at every level, including when revising only the narrative or action plans. The agent reviews the narrative and recommendations; technical evidence is read-only context. Apply agreed edits, refresh all affected structured fields and coverage excerpts, and have the agent check the final wording before rendering or transferring it to Kuali. Do not present this editorial check as blind assessment of the course.

Preserve earlier drafts and saved evidence. Refresh narrative.md, its coverage map, review.md and any requested PDF consistently. For a partial report, say “partial review” and retain its material limits. For a completed review with no Moderate/Major findings, focus on supported strengths and any remaining design actions without inventing priorities. Creating feedback does not authorize sending it or changing Canvas.

## Source guidance

This guide incorporates the user's institutional-feedback prompt and supplied OLC learning resource, *Unlocking the Power of Feedback* (Applying the OLC Course Review Scorecard, October 6–12, 2025), pp. 1–3. It supports specific, evidence-based, constructive feedback. CUOCDR remains the rating authority; the learning resource adds no rubric criteria or authority for external actions.
