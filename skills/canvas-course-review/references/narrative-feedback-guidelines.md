# Faculty-facing narrative feedback

Write as an institutional representative communicating the completed CUOCDR review to faculty, an instructional designer, or a dean. Use a professional, conversational tone: supportive, specific, non-confrontational, and focused on continued course improvement. Use plain faculty-facing language rather than reviewer jargon, unexplained rubric labels, scanner details, or internal source IDs. Retain conversational paragraphs around a short numbered list of priorities. Do not turn the overview into only a task list. Do not use checkboxes or a separate revision checklist.

Read and parse the finalized review.md and reconcile its findings with review.json when available. Use findings supported by inspected course evidence, explicit user verification, or user corrections to the review. Record the source and any limits in internal review artifacts; do not present user verification as a check the reviewer performed. Use positive comments only when supported by that evidence. Do not invent course features, student outcomes, instructor behavior, contacts, services, or promises. The narrative supplements the full criterion tables and does not change ratings. Place it near the top of review.md after course identity/status. Keep reviewer policy, scope rules, exceptions, audit history and verification provenance in internal artifacts or the report’s separate scope/evidence sections. Mention a limitation in the narrative only when it materially affects a finding or next step. A required partial-review disclosure is still necessary. It is a draft, not authorization to email faculty or post feedback.

## Report overview structure and grounding

- **Format:** set `narrative.format` to `report_overview`. The report header identifies the course; omit email subjects, greetings and sign-offs from the overview. Legacy saved email drafts remain supported, with their subject omitted when embedded in the report.
- **Opening paragraph:** thank the reader for the work represented in the course and describe specific strengths supported by Present ratings or positive reviewer comments. Explain their contribution to the intended learning experience without claiming measured outcomes.
- **Context paragraph and numbered priorities:** briefly explain the main concerns and their importance in conversational prose, then group related Moderate/Major findings into about three to five numbered themes; cover every such criterion. Give each theme a short bold label and links to the relevant detailed findings using `#cu-<id>`. Summarize the next step briefly in each theme; keep complete task/location instructions in the table action cells, as ordinary lists without checkboxes. Explain the supported finding, course context and why the change matters. Brief overlap between a priority summary and its detailed action is expected. Translate rubric wording into practical faculty-facing language. Preserve the finding's substance and scope, even when several criteria share a paragraph.
- **Next-term teaching tips:** add a short paragraph with at least three distinct, practical suggestions grounded in the reviewed course design and relevant interaction, feedback or assessment criteria. Frame them as options for the next offering, not evidence of past teaching, required repairs or reasons to change a rating. Use the same plain language as the rest of the narrative. For example, suggest using an existing discussion to surface questions, providing a feedback example before a major assignment, or inviting a brief midterm course-design check when the reviewed materials support those ideas. Do not invent course features or claim improved outcomes.
- **Closing paragraph:** end with one short, natural sentence relevant to this review. An institutional support offer is optional and should name a specific task only when supported by the context. Do not require “work in progress,” “continuous improvement,” repeated praise or a formulaic support promise. Do not invent staff, services or commitments.

When one evidenced conflict affects several criteria, describe one coordinated correction and link all of those findings. Name the types of course content that must agree when that helps explain the priority; keep the full list of destinations in the detailed table cells. Do not make one repair sound like several separate projects or combine unrelated tasks just because they share a theme. See [internal grouping guidance](internal-review-checks.md#2-group-related-findings-into-a-coordinated-repair).

Keep the complete overview at **600 words maximum**, including numbered priorities, teaching tips and closing; aim for about 300–450 words when the findings allow it. Use conversational opening/context/closing paragraphs and a scannable numbered priority list. Avoid dense rubric-by-rubric recitation. Keep the required tables for all 42 criteria in the seven categories; the overview supplements them. Prefer a few manageable, specific changes over a dense recitation of all minor findings, but never omit a Moderate/Major item to shorten the draft.

## Plain language

Apply this language standard to every recommendation the end user reads: the narrative, action/verification columns, instructor preparation reminders, link labels, and PDF. The numbered narrative list gives brief priority summaries and next steps; the detailed tables give complete task/location instructions. Do not add a duplicate revision checklist. Keep report-level scope and limitations outside the narrative unless a limitation changes a finding or next step. Keep the original rubric wording unchanged.

Write the way an institutional colleague would explain the findings in a normal conversation. Name the actual task and course location instead of compressing them into abstract labels. Prefer familiar verbs such as add, check, explain, revise, and publish. Avoid forced compound modifiers, strings of adjectives, and phrases that make ordinary work sound more academic or technical than it is. Institutional voice does not mean formal or stuffy language.

Translate technical evidence into its practical meaning. For example, write “the videos have captions, but we still need to check their accuracy” instead of “caption tracks remain unverified”; “ask CTL to confirm the university’s requirements for accessible tables” instead of “obtain table guidance”; “add instructions for filing a complaint” instead of “add a grievance-filing route”. Routine preparation reminders belong outside the narrative, so do not add one merely to use simpler wording. Explain that a screen reader needs to recognize a table’s column headings rather than asking faculty to make “grading-table headers programmatic.” Technical terms needed to carry out a specific task belong with a brief explanation or in the detailed report, not in an unexplained list in the overview.

During the final read, ask whether a faculty member unfamiliar with the review process could understand each sentence and know what to do next. Rewrite any sentence that requires decoding. Keep the evidence, uncertainty, and recommendation intact while simplifying the wording; plain language must not weaken the finding.

## Priority, specificity and scope

Order themes implicitly: **accessibility/compliance**, then **student learning impact**, then **reviewer emphasis**. Within that ordering, begin with readily achievable changes when evidence supports their usefulness; do not let easy cosmetic edits displace an accessibility barrier or a more consequential learning issue. Do not promise effort estimates or improved performance without evidence. For each theme, explain the practical impact and at least one concrete next step; each mapped Moderate/Major finding must retain its own actionable treatment. Link summaries to detailed findings rather than repeating every individual task.

Distinguish confirmed deficiencies from unresolved verification needs. State material access/policy limits without asserting violations, universal accessibility, or release approval. Scope historical observations to the saved review: do not imply a new live check. For master/blueprint templates, intentional instructor-completion scaffolds count as Present rather than deficiencies. Keep routine biography, contact, publication and other preparation reminders in the detailed report or preparation notes, not the narrative. Mention preparation work in the narrative only when it addresses a confirmed design finding; do not let a routine reminder obscure that finding.

Keep scored findings within the design-only scope in [review policy](review-policy.md). Assess whether written interaction and feedback plans are clear and consistent, without judging how an instructor carried them out. The next-term teaching tips are unscored suggestions and remain separate from confirmed revisions and the Moderate/Major coverage map.

## Final verification

Build an internal coverage map from each finalized Moderate/Major ID to the exact narrative passage and concrete next step. Identify the thanks/strengths opening, at least three distinct next-term teaching tips, and brief, natural closing; check that the tips are practical suggestions grounded in the design rather than judgments about past teaching. Check themes, implicit priority, evidence/context/impact, institutional voice, plain language, actionable recommendations, template treatment, and the complete word count manually. Confirm that user verification is recorded internally, routine preparation reminders are omitted, summaries are not full duplicated action lists, and the narrative does not explain internal guidelines. Run report validation where structured artifacts exist; it cannot judge substantive tone or completeness by itself.

Preserve earlier drafts and refresh narrative.md, its coverage map, review.md, and any requested/delivered PDF consistently. Keep private report/evidence artifacts ignored. If a report is partial, explicitly say partial review and retain its limits. If there are no Moderate/Major findings, say that only when the completed review supports it and focus on evidenced strengths and any remaining design actions.

## Source guidance

This guidance incorporates the user's institutional-feedback prompt and the supplied OLC learning resource, *Unlocking the Power of Feedback* (Applying the OLC Course Review Scorecard, October 6–12, 2025), pp. 1–3. The resource emphasizes supportive specificity, rubric/objective alignment, balanced recognition and critique, evidence-led themes, useful achievable changes, and iterative improvement. It informs writing practice; CUOCDR remains the rating authority, and the supplied document does not authorize external actions or add rubric criteria.
