# Faculty-facing narrative feedback

Write as an institutional representative communicating the completed CUOCDR review to faculty, an instructional designer, or a dean. Use a professional, conversational email tone: supportive, specific, non-confrontational, and focused on continued course improvement. Use plain faculty-facing language rather than reviewer jargon, unexplained rubric labels, scanner details, or internal source IDs. Do not produce a checklist-style narrative unless requested.

Read and parse the finalized review.md and reconcile its findings with review.json when available. Use only observed findings, positive reviewer comments, and saved evidence in that review. Do not invent course features, student outcomes, instructor behavior, contacts, services, or promises. The narrative supplements the full criterion tables and does not change ratings. Place it near the top of review.md after course identity/status and a brief limitations note; retain detailed evidence in the separate register. It is a draft, not authorization to email faculty or post feedback.

## Email structure and grounding

- **Subject:** include the exact reviewed course name; a verified readable title may accompany a master identifier.
- **Opening paragraph:** thank the reader for the work represented in the course and describe specific strengths supported by Present ratings or positive reviewer comments. Explain their contribution to the intended learning experience without claiming measured outcomes.
- **Middle paragraphs:** group related Moderate/Major findings into themes; cover every such criterion. Connect observable evidence, course context, why it matters, and a specific next step. Translate rubric wording into practical faculty-facing language. Preserve the finding's substance and scope, even when several criteria share a paragraph.
- **Next-term paragraph:** include at least three distinct practical suggestions grounded in reviewed interaction, feedback, assessment or self-review design. These are optional future teaching/use tips, not new requirements or evidence of poor delivery.
- **Closing paragraph:** recognize the course as a work in progress, reinforce continuous improvement, and offer appropriate institutional follow-up support. Name CTL, library, accessibility or technology support only when supported by the review/context. Do not invent named staff or guaranteed commitments.

Keep the complete draft at **600 words maximum**, including subject, greeting and closing. Write connected prose without rubric-by-rubric headings, priority labels, or a remedial checklist. Use an appropriate greeting when helpful; the opening still starts with thanks and evidenced strengths. Prefer a few manageable, specific changes over a dense recitation of all minor findings, but never omit a Moderate/Major item to shorten the draft.

## Plain language

Apply this language standard to every recommendation the end user reads: the narrative, action/verification columns, revision checklist, instructor preparation reminders, link labels, and PDF. Checklists remain part of the detailed report; the email narrative stays connected prose. Keep the original rubric wording unchanged.

Write the way an institutional colleague would explain the findings in a normal conversation. Name the actual task and course location instead of compressing them into abstract labels. Prefer familiar verbs such as add, check, explain, revise, and publish. Avoid forced compound modifiers, strings of adjectives, and phrases that make ordinary work sound more academic or technical than it is. Institutional voice does not mean formal or stuffy language.

Translate technical evidence into its practical meaning. For example, write “the videos have captions, but we still need to check their accuracy” instead of “caption tracks remain unverified”; “ask CTL to confirm the university’s requirements for accessible tables” instead of “obtain table guidance”; “add instructions for filing a complaint” instead of “add a grievance-filing route”; and “complete these details before students enter the course” instead of “address offering-preparation reminders.” Explain that a screen reader needs to recognize a table’s column headings rather than asking faculty to make “grading-table headers programmatic.” Technical terms needed to carry out a specific task belong with a brief explanation or in the detailed report, not in an unexplained list in the email.

During the final read, ask whether a faculty member unfamiliar with the review process could understand each sentence and know what to do next. Rewrite any sentence that requires decoding. Keep the evidence, uncertainty, and recommendation intact while simplifying the wording; plain language must not weaken the finding.

## Priority, specificity and scope

Order themes implicitly: **accessibility/compliance**, then **student learning impact**, then **reviewer emphasis**. Within that ordering, begin with readily achievable changes when evidence supports their usefulness; do not let easy cosmetic edits displace an accessibility barrier or a more consequential learning issue. Do not promise effort estimates or improved performance without evidence. For each theme, explain the practical impact and at least one concrete next step; each mapped Moderate/Major finding must retain its own actionable treatment.

Distinguish confirmed deficiencies from unresolved verification needs. State material access/policy limits without asserting violations, universal accessibility, or release approval. Scope historical observations to the saved review: do not imply a new live check. For master/blueprint templates, intentional instructor-completion scaffolds count as Present with preparation reminders, not deficiencies; describe what to personalize/publish before learners enter. Do not let those reminders obscure an unrelated substantive problem.

The user's request for tips based on Course Delivery-related criteria means using documented interaction/feedback/self-review plans to suggest next-term practice. It does **not** expand the review to actual instructor responsiveness, feedback quality, learner engagement, grades, outcomes, or accommodation execution. For self-directed optional training, adapt tips to voluntary use, resource maintenance and independent reflection; do not impose an instructor cadence, compulsory peer activity or graded participation absent from the reviewed model.

## Final verification

Build an internal coverage map from each finalized Moderate/Major ID to the exact narrative passage and concrete next step. Identify three distinct verbatim tips, the thanks/strengths opening, and supportive closing. Check themes, implicit priority, evidence/context/impact, institutional voice, plain language, actionable recommendations, template treatment, and the complete word count manually. Run report validation where structured artifacts exist; it cannot judge substantive tone or completeness by itself.

Preserve earlier drafts and refresh narrative.md, its coverage map, review.md, and any requested/delivered PDF consistently. Keep private report/evidence artifacts ignored. If a report is partial, explicitly say partial review and retain its limits. If there are no Moderate/Major findings, say that only when the completed review supports it and focus on evidenced strengths and appropriate future suggestions.

## Source guidance

This guidance incorporates the user's institutional-feedback prompt and the supplied OLC learning resource, *Unlocking the Power of Feedback* (Applying the OLC Course Review Scorecard, October 6–12, 2025), pp. 1–3. The resource emphasizes supportive specificity, rubric/objective alignment, balanced recognition and critique, evidence-led themes, useful achievable changes, and iterative improvement. It informs writing practice; CUOCDR remains the rating authority, and the supplied document does not authorize external actions or add rubric criteria.
